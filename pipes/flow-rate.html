How it works

Flow velocity follows the continuity equation: velocity equals flow rate divided by the pipe's cross-sectional area.

v = Q / A, where A = π × (D / 2)²

Q is flow rate, D is inside diameter, and A is the circular cross-sectional area. A smaller diameter forces the same flow through a smaller area, so velocity rises.

Worked example

10 GPM through a 1 in inside-diameter pipe: area ≈ 0.785 in², so converting units gives a velocity of about 4.1 ft/s — comfortably under an 8 ft/s guideline. Dropping to a 0.5 in pipe at the same flow rate roughly quadruples the velocity to around 16 ft/s, well over the guideline.

Assumptions

  • Flow is treated as steady and fills the full pipe cross-section (no partial flow or free surface).
  • "Inside diameter" is the actual bore, which is smaller than the nominal pipe size for most schedules.
  • The velocity limit is a general guideline, not a code requirement for every application.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate flow velocity in a pipe?

Divide the flow rate by the pipe's cross-sectional area. A smaller diameter at the same flow rate always gives a higher velocity.

What is a safe pipe velocity for water?

A common guideline is about 8 ft/s (2.4 m/s) or less in distribution piping, to limit erosion, noise, and water-hammer risk.

Does this calculator account for pressure drop?

No — use the dedicated pressure-drop calculator for Darcy-Weisbach pressure loss and head loss.
